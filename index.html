How fast technology spreads, and who gains power from it

Much of the debate about chips and AI treats them as a race to build the most advanced version first. This study asks a slightly different question: does it matter more how quickly a country gets the technology into the hands of ordinary businesses?

The question comes from the political scientist Jeffrey Ding, who has argued from history that it does. Here his idea is tested on thirty years of World Bank data for 123 economies, and then carried forward to AI. The answer turns into a model you can run yourself, with the United States, China and India at its centre.

The short version

In the internet era, the countries where digital technology spread fastest went on to win a bigger slice of the world's factory output. The countries that adopted early saw their workers become more productive, and those same countries are the heavier users of AI today. Spending more on research, or filing more patents, did not go with a bigger share of world power once a country's income and investment were taken into account.

Shares of world GDP and defence spending mostly followed two forces: poorer economies catching up and economies that invest heavily. China's high investment rate explains much of its projected lead, and tightening its access to chips narrows that lead by about three points.

Chapter 2

Two ways a technology can shift the balance of power

Some technologies end up in almost every part of the economy. Electricity was one, and the semiconductor chip is another. Economists call these general-purpose technologies, and AI is widely expected to become the next one.

Leading at the frontier

The usual view is that the country which invents a new technology first, and makes the most of it, earns early profits in a few new industries and grows stronger on the back of them. In this study that view is measured through research spending, patents, scientific papers, and each country's share of world electronics production.

Spreading it widely

Ding's view is that power shifts toward the country where the technology reaches the most firms, early and quickly, so that productivity rises across many industries at once. He traces Britain's rise in the first industrial revolution and America's in the second to this. Here it is measured through the shape of each country's digital adoption curve.

Adoption always follows an S-shaped curve

A new technology starts with a few users, then spreads quickly once enough people have it, and finally levels off when nearly everyone does. Any such curve can be described with two numbers. The midpoint is the year adoption crosses half the population, so an earlier midpoint means a country got going sooner. The speed is how steeply the curve climbs once it is under way. Move the sliders to draw your own curve, and compare it with the actual internet data for a country.

How the idea was put to the test

Gathering the data

The study uses the World Bank's World Development Indicators for every economy with more than four million people, from 1995 to 2024. Each year's figures were averaged into five-year blocks, so one unusually good or bad year does not throw the picture off.

Measuring power as a share of the world

Power is relative, so each country's weight is measured as its share of the world total: of manufacturing output, of GDP adjusted for price differences, of goods exports and of military spending. Output per worker is tracked too, because Ding's argument runs through productivity. If the world makes 100 units of manufactured goods and a country makes 30 of them, its share is 30 per cent.

Drawing an S-curve for every country

Internet use is the broadest record available of how far chip-based technology has spread. Fitting an S-curve to each country's internet data gives its midpoint and its speed. The curves fit closely; in a typical country they account for about 99 per cent of the ups and downs in the data.

Letting the data choose between explanations

For each measure of power the study tried several possible formulas and kept the one that predicted best. The test was strict: each formula was worked out with one country left out, then asked to predict that country, and this was repeated for every country in turn. The row of dots below shows the country being left out moving along the sample.

Writing the predictions down first

Before any of the outcome data were downloaded, the study recorded what it expected to find. The first test used a simple snapshot of how digitised each country was, and it found nothing. The S-curve approach came after that, which is why the paper treats its results as findings to check again once data for 2025–29 become available.

The equations as a machine

The simulator draws these equations in the style of system-dynamics software such as Vensim. Each country's shares sit in the tanks on the right. The valves in the pipes set how fast each tank fills or drains every year, and the circles on the left are the inputs that turn the valves. A blue arrow opens a valve and a red arrow closes it, with thicker arrows pushing harder. The dashed gold loop is the catch-up effect: as a country's GDP share grows its income rises, and richer countries grow more slowly. The diagram below shows India in 2025.

Try an equation yourself

Pick an equation and move the inputs. The bars show how much each input adds or takes away, and together they make up the predicted twenty-year change. Inputs that are not part of the chosen equation are greyed out.

Predicted twenty-year change

A worked example with India's own numbers

Between 2000–04 and 2020–24, India's scores were +0.97 for adoption speed, +0.48 for timing, +0.49 for electronics production, −0.79 for income and +1.55 for investment. Putting these into the productivity equation gives:

0.452 − 0.005×0.97 − 0.217×0.48 + 0.080×0.49 − 0.475×(−0.79) + 0.086×1.55 = 0.893

India's actual change was 0.897, so output per worker grew about two and a half times over those two decades and the equation comes very close. For China the same equation gives 1.17 against an actual 1.55. The equation describes a typical country, and China's productivity gain went well beyond it.

Chapter 7

Data, sources and limits

All the numbers on this site come from public sources. Each variable below links to its page on the World Bank's data portal.

Other sources

The Microsoft AI Diffusion Report for the second half of 2025 estimates the share of working-age adults using generative AI in 147 economies. Twenty-nine of them carry identical regional estimates and were left out.

The Stanford AI Index 2025 counts notable AI models (40 from the US and 15 from China in 2024) and private AI investment ($109.1 billion in the US and $9.3 billion in China).

China's integrated-circuit trade for 2015–2025 comes from the General Administration of Customs of China, accessed through FactIQ.

Jeffrey Ding's argument is set out in Technology and the Rise of Great Powers (Princeton University Press, 2024) and in his article in International Studies Quarterly.

The study also draws on Comin and Hobijn (2010) on how long technologies take to spread between countries, and Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson (2021) on the delayed productivity payoff from such technologies.

It connects to the Takshashila discussion document A Strategic Assessment of Technology Denial Regimes (February 2026).

What the data cannot tell us

The results are patterns across countries over a single twenty-year stretch. They allow for income and investment, and they hold up when China is removed or when the sample is reshuffled 2,000 times, but patterns of this kind cannot prove cause and effect on their own.

Internet use is an imperfect stand-in for how far chip-based technology has spread inside firms, and the electronics production measure counts assembly and re-export alongside chip-making. Taiwan, which matters a great deal for chips, is missing from World Bank data.

The AI figures come from one company's estimates for one period and may understate use in China. The projections carry forward the relationships of 2000–24, and a new technology could change them.

The first test, written down in advance, found nothing, and the S-curve approach was adopted after it. Its results should be checked again on data for 2025–29.

Chapter 8

Using this site

The chapters are meant to be read in order the first time through. After that, most readers will want to spend their time in the simulator.

Moving around

The chapter names at the top switch between chapters, and each chapter ends with a link to the next one. Every chapter has its own web address, so you can share a link that opens straight to, say, the simulator. Hovering over a line, dot or bar shows its exact value; on a phone, tap it instead. The small i markers next to controls explain what each one does.

Sliders and scenarios

Drag a slider, or click it and use the arrow keys. Most simulator sliders move an input up or down in standard deviations, a measure of how far apart countries usually are. A shift of +1 is large; it is roughly the gap between an average country and one in the top sixth. Scenarios set several sliders at once to a case discussed in the paper, and you can keep adjusting from there.

Running the simulator

Set up puts the clock back to 2025. Run moves forward one year at a time until 2050, and pressing it again pauses. Step one year moves the clock by a single year, and the speed slider sets how quickly it ticks. If you change a slider while the clock is running, the whole path is worked out again straight away and the clock stays where it is.

Reading the diagram and plots

In the diagram the tank levels rise and fall with each country's shares, the moving dashes in the pipes show the direction and pace of each flow, and the label above each valve gives the yearly growth rate. The buttons above the diagram choose which country it shows. Below the plots, the text describes your pathway and the bar chart shows which of your changes mattered most.

A few terms

A share of the world is a country's portion of a global total. The composite is the average of four such shares. A log change of 0.10 is about a 10 per cent rise and 0.69 is a doubling. Diffusion is the spread of a technology from its first users to most of the economy.

Hosting and data

The whole site is one file with its data built in, so it runs on GitHub Pages without any setup. The data files and the scripts used to produce the estimates are in the replication package that accompanies the paper.
