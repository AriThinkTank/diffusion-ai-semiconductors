How fast technology spreads, and who gains power from it

This site tests an idea from the political scientist Jeffrey Ding against thirty years of data on 123 economies, applies it to semiconductors and artificial intelligence, and turns the results into a model that anyone can run. It covers the United States, China and India.

The story in brief

Governments treat chips and AI as a race to the frontier. Ding argues, from history, that economic leadership has gone to the economies where a foundational technology spread earliest and fastest through ordinary businesses.

The data for the internet era agree on the parts of power closest to production. Faster spread went with larger gains in world manufacturing share. Earlier spread went with faster productivity growth, and it carries into AI: countries that went digital early use more AI today. Research spending and patents showed no link to gains in any share of world power.

Shares of world GDP and defence spending moved with catch-up growth and investment. China's high investment rate drives much of its projected lead. A severe squeeze on China's access to chips narrows that lead modestly and does not reverse it.

Where to go next

The idea and how it was tested

A general-purpose technology is one used across almost every part of the economy, the way electricity once was. Semiconductors are one. AI is widely expected to be the next.

Pathway 1: leading the frontier

The economy that invents a new technology first and makes the most of it earns early profits in a few new industries, and gains power from them. Measured here by research spending, patents, scientific papers and the share of world electronics production.

Pathway 2: spreading the technology

The economy where the technology reaches the most firms, early and fast, sees productivity rise across many industries, and gains power from that. Ding argues this pathway explains Britain's rise in the first industrial revolution and America's in the second. Measured here by the shape of each country's digital adoption curve.

Every adoption follows an S-curve. Try drawing one.

Adoption starts slowly, speeds up, then levels off as almost everyone has the technology. Two numbers describe any S-curve. The midpoint is the year adoption crosses half the population; an earlier midpoint means earlier diffusion. The speed is how steeply the curve climbs. Move the sliders and compare your curve with the actual internet data for three countries.

How the test worked, step by step

Collect official data for 123 economies

All economies with more than four million people, from the World Bank's World Development Indicators, 1995 to 2024. Yearly values were averaged into six five-year windows so that a single good or bad year does not distort the picture.

Measure five bases of power as shares of the world

Share of world manufacturing, of world GDP (adjusted for price differences), of world goods exports and of world military spending, plus output per worker. Power is relative, so shares are the natural unit. Example: if the world makes 100 units of manufactured goods and a country makes 30, its share is 30 per cent.

Fit an S-curve to each country's internet use

Internet use is the broadest available record of how far chip-based digital technology spread. For every country, the curve gives a midpoint and a speed. The fits are close: in a typical country the curve explains 99 per cent of the movement in the data.

Put every measure on one scale

Each input is expressed in standard deviations (SD) from the average country. A score of 0 is average; +1 is well above average, roughly among the top sixth; −1 is well below. This lets research spending and adoption speed be compared fairly. Example: India's internet speed score is +0.97, so India's adoption climbed faster than most countries.

Relate inputs to the 20-year change in each share

The change from 2000–04 to 2020–24 is measured on a log scale. A change of 0.10 means about 10 per cent growth in the share; 0.69 means it doubled; −0.69 means it halved. Twenty years matches Ding's point that a new technology takes decades to pay off.

Let the data choose the formula

Seven candidate formulas were tested for each base of power. Each was fitted with one country held out, used to predict that country, and repeated for every country. The formula with the smallest prediction error was kept. Watch the held-out country move along the row below.

Write the hypotheses down before looking

Five predictions were recorded before any outcome data were downloaded. The first test, which used a snapshot of how digitised each country was, came back empty, and the paper reports that. The S-curve approach came after, so its results should be checked again when data for 2025–29 arrive.

The formula, in plain language

The model is called the Diffusion–Power Equations. It is five short recipes, one for each base of power. Each recipe says how much a country's share is likely to grow over twenty years, given a handful of facts about it.

20-year change in a share = starting drift + (weight × input) + (weight × input) + …

The inputs

Seven facts about each economy, each scored in standard deviations from average: κ speed of adoption, τ timing of adoption (higher = later), P share of world electronics production, R research spending, A scientific papers per person, Y income per person, K investment rate.

The weights

Numbers produced by the data. Each says how much the 20-year change moves when that input rises by one standard deviation. A positive weight pushes a share up; a negative weight pulls it down. A blank means the data showed the input did not help predict that share.

The starting drift

What an exactly average country would see. Because small economies grew faster than large ones over 2000–24, the drift is positive for most shares.

The five recipes at a glance

Blue cells push a share up, red cells pull it down, and darker colour means a stronger push. Hover for the exact weight and its 95 per cent range.

The machine behind the simulator

The simulator draws the equations as a stock-and-flow diagram, the convention used in system-dynamics tools such as Vensim. The tanks on the right are stocks: each country's share of world manufacturing, GDP, exports and military spending, and its productivity. The valves in the pipes are flows: how fast each tank fills or drains each year. The circles on the left are the inputs, and the arrows show which input feeds which valve. Blue arrows open a valve, red arrows close it. The loop at the bottom is a feedback: as a country's GDP share grows, its income rises, and richer economies catch up more slowly.

Predicted 20-year change

A worked example: India's productivity, 2000–24

India's scores were: speed +0.97, timing +0.48, electronics production +0.49, income −0.79, investment +1.55. The productivity recipe gives:

0.452 − 0.005×0.97 − 0.217×0.48 + 0.080×0.49 − 0.475×(−0.79) + 0.086×1.55 = 0.893

India's actual change was 0.897, which means output per worker grew about 2.45 times over the two decades. The same recipe gives 1.17 for China against an actual 1.55. The recipe describes the typical economy, and China's productivity gain went beyond it.

From twenty-year changes to a year-by-year path

One step at a time

Each year a share is multiplied by the exponential of one-twentieth of its 20-year change: share next year = share this year × exp(change ÷ 20).

Catch-up slows

When a country's GDP share rises, its income score rises by the log change in that share divided by 1.27, so later catch-up is slower. This matches what happened to Japan and South Korea.

The rest of the world

The rest of the world holds whatever the three economies do not, and never less than 5 per cent. Inputs stay within the range seen in the data, so the formula is never pushed into territory it has not seen.

Composite share = ¼ × (GDP share + manufacturing share + export share + military share)

For AI, the same equations are carried forward. The data support this: AI use today follows the timing of the earlier digital spread closely (finding F5).

What the data cannot show

The results are patterns across countries over one 20-year period. They control for income and investment and hold when China is removed or the sample is resampled 2,000 times, but they cannot by themselves prove cause and effect.

Internet use is an imperfect stand-in for chip-based technology inside firms. The electronics production measure counts assembly and re-export as well as chip-making. Taiwan is missing from World Bank data.

The AI figures come from one company's telemetry for one period and may understate use in China. Projections extend the 2000–24 relationships; a new technology could change them.

The first, pre-registered test was null, and the S-curve design came after it. Its results should be confirmed on 2025–29 data.

How to use this site

Each tab does one job. Read them in order the first time; after that, the Simulator is the place to explore.

Tabs

Click a tab name at the top to switch. Each tab has its own web address, so a link to a tab opens that tab directly.

Hovering

Move the pointer over any line, dot or bar to see its exact value. On a phone, tap instead.

The i markers

Hover over or tab to an i marker next to a control to read what it does and what range is sensible.

Sliders

Drag a slider or use the arrow keys after clicking it. The number beside it shows the current setting. Shifts are in standard deviations: +1 is a large change, roughly what separates an average country from one among the top sixth.

Presets

A preset sets several sliders at once to a scenario discussed in the paper, such as a severe chip squeeze on China. After choosing one you can keep adjusting.

Set up, Run and Step

Set up puts the clock back to 2025 and fills the tanks with 2020–24 values. Run advances one year at a time until 2050; press it again to pause. Step one year moves forward by a single year. Run speed sets how fast the clock ticks. Changing a slider recalculates the whole pathway at once, and the clock stays where it is.

Monitors, diagram and plots

The monitors show the year and the composite shares at the current tick. In the diagram, tank levels rise and fall; valve labels give the yearly growth rate; arrow thickness shows how strongly each input is pushing at that moment. Pick which economy the diagram shows with the buttons above it. The plots trace solid lines for your pathway against dashed baseline lines.

Reading the results

Below the plots, the text describes your pathway, the bar chart shows which of your changes moved the 2045 China–US gap and by how much, and the cards list the recommendations linked to your settings.

Glossary

Share of the world: a country's portion of a global total. Composite: the average of four shares. Log change: 0.10 ≈ +10%, 0.69 = doubled. Standard deviation: the typical spread across countries. Diffusion: the spread of a technology from early users to most of the economy.
